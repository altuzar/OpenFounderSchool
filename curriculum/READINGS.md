# Eight original working readings

These explanations were created for OpenFounderSchool, not summarized from books or transcripts. Use them in 20-minute blocks: read, explain in your own words, and apply to the week's deliverable. Discuss orally or through drawings if useful. No purchases or accounts are required.

## A. An idea versus a problem

“I want to build an app” describes an object. “My family member spends twenty minutes finding which product is in storage” describes an observable situation. An app could be one solution, but so could a label, list, or different task sequence.

Before selecting a solution, find out who experiences the situation, when it last happened, and what they do now. Do not just ask whether your idea is good: people can be kind without needing it. Look for cases where the problem does not occur too. A useful hypothesis can be false.

Exercise: turn three ideas into observable situations. For one, write what evidence would make you abandon it. Do not call this research yet: it remains a hypothesis.

## B. Levels of evidence

A positive opinion shows that someone said something positive. It does not demonstrate purchase, use, or benefit. A person trying to complete a task provides another signal; a specific commitment another; payment for a delivered result another. None removes every doubt on its own.

Record evidence with its limits: anonymized participant, behavior, conditions, and what you could not verify. If you only tested with family, say so. If AI played the customer, write SIMULATION. If someone promised to buy and did not, record both.

Exercise: sort five signals from a project by what they actually demonstrate. Then imagine how each could mislead you. A smaller honest test is often better than invented certainty.

## C. An offer is a commitment

An offer connects buyer, situation, delivery, deadline, price, and boundaries. “I help with your social media” leaves many questions. “I prepare three draft posts from your notes for review on Friday” makes fulfillment checkable. This example does not authorize using private notes or sending content.

If you add a remedy for nonperformance, calculate how you could honor it several times in a row. Do not promise outcomes outside your control. Ensuring a draft follows a format differs from guaranteeing sales. An unclear or impossible remedy only adds risk.

Exercise: write a fictional offer and ask someone to repeat it in their own words. If they understand something else, improve it before considering a sale.

## D. The number that looks like profit

Receiving money feels like winning, but some may already be committed to materials, tools, refunds, or taxes. Investment also reaches the account without being a sale. An owner withdrawal moves cash but does not itself create a new delivery cost.

Start by classifying every movement. Ask what costs are missing and which period you are measuring. Cash-basis records are an initial aid; inventory, assets, and unpaid invoices require further adjustments to understand economic results. Do not hide that distinction to display an attractive figure.

Exercise: invent a business with sales of 100 and costs of 70. Add a refund of 20 and financing of 500. Explain why money received and the operating result tell different stories. Check docs/FINANCE.md.

## E. AI as a fast apprentice

AI can produce an answer before you understand the question. That helps generate examples but becomes risky if speed replaces verification. A small specification helps: what it should do, with which data, under what restrictions, and how you will detect failure.

Request a solution to a simple case and test an empty and incorrect case. Explain the result yourself. If you cannot understand it, reduce the problem. Record your contribution and the AI's. Using eight agents where one is enough does not add learning.

Exercise: instruct the AI to classify five fictional tasks. Add an ambiguous task and observe whether it invents certainty. Change the instruction to allow “I don't know” and compare.

## F. Distribution without intrusion

A useful product needs to reach someone, but that does not require contacting everyone. Begin with a relevant person approved by your adult. Explain why it might matter, what you are asking, and how they can say no. Do not turn an interview into a surprise sales pitch.

A post can show your reasoning without exposing your private life. Share a synthetic example and credit what you learned from others. Citing someone famous does not imply endorsement. Followers and views are not profit or customer approval.

Exercise: draft a fictional message for someone with the problem and another for someone without it. Explain why sending the second would be a poor test even if it got more replies.

## G. A system can cost money while you are not looking

A manual task runs when someone starts it. A scheduled task can keep working while you sleep or nobody uses the product. A small write can trigger other reads too. Counting only the initial operation does not reveal total usage.

Before automating, measure one execution, the empty case, and failure. Define an owner, budget, and stop mechanism. For a demo, use local data and bounded execution. Deleting records later does not automatically recover consumed compute or calls.

Exercise: compare a manual ten-row report with one running every minute for thirty days. The second means 43,200 monthly executions before downstream work. Then decide what information you actually need.

## H. Knowing when to close is part of building

An idea can teach you and still not be worth continuing. The cost is more than money: it includes time for another test, rest, or school. Decide beforehand what signal supports continuation and what would make you stop.

When closing, fulfill commitments, export what is needed, reduce access, and cancel unused services. Keep a note of what you learned and what to check if you return. Evidence-based closure teaches more than maintaining a fiction of success.

Exercise: write one argument for continuing your project and one for closing it. Ask someone to argue against your preferred option. Decide without using the million-dollar goal as the only reason.
